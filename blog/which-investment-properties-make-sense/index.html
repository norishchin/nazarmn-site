Which investment properties make sense, and which ones don't

A building that looks great on the listing can lose money every month. Here is how I separate the deals from the traps, with a worked example.

I get sent a lot of "great deals." Most of them are great for the seller. The difference between a property that builds wealth and one that drains it usually comes down to a few honest numbers that the listing doesn't show you.

Start with what it really costs to own

Listings show rent and taxes. Real ownership also includes insurance, repairs, big-ticket replacements like roofs and boilers, empty months between tenants, and either a property manager or your own evenings. If you leave those out, every building looks like a winner.

A worked example: one fourplex, two prices

These numbers are made up for illustration, not a quote on any real building, and the 7% rate is just an assumption. The point is the method.

The building: four units renting for $1,450 each, so $5,800 a month in rent.

Monthly expenses:

  • Vacancy at 5% of rent: $290
  • Property taxes: $750
  • Insurance: $350
  • Repairs and future replacements at 10% of rent: $580
  • Management at 8% of rent: $464
  • Owner-paid water, sewer and trash: $300

That's $2,734 a month, leaving $3,066 of net operating income, or about $36,800 a year. That number doesn't change with the price you pay. What changes is the loan.

At the $650,000 asking price, with 25% down ($162,500) and a 30-year loan at 7%, the mortgage is about $3,243 a month. You lose about $177 every month. The cap rate is about 5.7%, and the income covers the loan only 0.95 times.

At $575,000, the mortgage drops to about $2,869 and the building makes about $197 a month. Coverage rises to about 1.07.

Or keep $650,000 and change the terms. If the seller carries the loan at 5%, the payment is about $2,617 and the building makes about $449 a month.

Same building, same rents. Price and terms decide whether it works.

Three numbers I check on every deal

  • Cash flow: what's left each month after every expense and the mortgage. If it's negative, you're paying for the privilege of owning it.
  • Cap rate: net operating income divided by price. It lets you compare buildings of different sizes and prices on equal footing.
  • Debt coverage: net operating income divided by the loan payment. Lenders want to see room above 1.0, and so should you.

Deals that usually make sense

  • Small multifamily you can live in. Living in one unit of a duplex to fourplex while renting the others is one of the best ways to start.
  • Value-add with a clear scope. Rents below market for a reason you can fix and price: dated kitchens, poor management, units sitting empty.
  • Good terms from a motivated seller. Seller carry or contract for deed can turn a thin deal into a solid one. (See how contract for deed works.)
  • Boring locations with steady demand. Close to jobs, transit and schools. Boring rents well.

Deals that usually don't

  • Negative cash flow on the promise of appreciation. Appreciation is a bonus, not a plan.
  • "Pro forma" rents. Underwrite the rent roll that exists today, not the one in the seller's spreadsheet.
  • Deferred maintenance you can't price. An old boiler or a flat roof at the end of its life can erase years of cash flow. Get bids before you commit.
  • Short-term rental math in a town that limits short-term rentals. Check the city's rules and licensing before you count that income.
  • A price set by the seller's mortgage balance. What they owe has nothing to do with what it's worth.
