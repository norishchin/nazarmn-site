Contract for deed in Minnesota: how it works and when it makes sense

Seller financing can close a deal a bank won't. It also carries real risk on both sides. A plain-English walkthrough.

A contract for deed is seller financing. The buyer makes payments to the seller, not to a bank, and gets the deed once the contract is paid off. I use it in the right situations for homes, small apartment buildings, and commercial property.

How it works

  1. Buyer and seller agree on price, down payment, interest rate, monthly payment, and term.
  2. The buyer moves in or takes over the building and pays the seller every month. The buyer usually covers taxes, insurance, and repairs.
  3. Many contracts end with a balloon: the remaining balance is due on a set date, often paid by refinancing with a bank.
  4. When the balance is paid, the seller signs over the deed.

When it makes sense

  • The buyer is self-employed, new to the country, or has income a bank underwrites poorly, but has a solid down payment.
  • The property is hard to finance: mixed use, a building that needs work, a special-use property like a church.
  • The seller owns it free and clear and wants steady income more than a lump sum.

The risks, plainly

For buyers: if you miss payments, the seller can cancel the contract through a legal notice process that moves much faster than a bank foreclosure. You could lose the property and what you've paid in. If a balloon is coming, you need a real plan to refinance before it arrives.

For sellers: you are the bank. If the buyer stops paying, you take the property back in whatever condition it is in. If you still have a mortgage on it, your lender may have the right to call the loan due.

How to do it right

  • Have a real estate attorney draft or review the contract. Minnesota has specific rules for these, and the state tightened them in 2024, especially for investors who sell this way often.
  • Close at a title company and record the contract with the county.
  • Get a title search and an inspection, same as any purchase.
  • Write down who pays taxes and insurance, and how the seller will get proof.
  • Underwrite the balloon. If the plan is to refinance in five years, talk to a lender now about what that will take.
