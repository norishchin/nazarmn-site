How off-market commercial deals actually happen

Most small apartment buildings and commercial properties never get a sign in the yard. They change hands through research and phone calls.

Many apartment buildings, strip centers, and warehouses in Minnesota sell without ever being listed. The owner gets a call, has a conversation, and a deal comes together quietly. Here is what that looks like from the inside.

It starts with the owner, not the property

Off-market work is research and phone calls. County records, rental licenses, and business filings tell you who owns a building and for how long. Then you call. Most owners say no. Some say "not now." A few say "what would you pay?" That is the whole job, repeated.

Why owners sell this way

  • Privacy. Tenants and employees don't find out from a sign.
  • Simplicity. One serious buyer, no parade of showings.
  • Terms. A direct conversation leaves room for seller carry, a longer close, or a sale structured around taxes.

What a buyer needs before the call comes

When I bring a deal to an investor, the ones who win are ready. They have proof of funds or a lender who knows them, a clear picture of what they buy, and the ability to answer in days. An off-market seller picked a quiet sale to avoid hassle. Slow buyers are hassle.

Underwrite it like no one else has

A listed property comes with a marketing package. An off-market one comes with whatever the owner hands you. I rebuild the numbers from the rent roll, actual expenses, and tax records, then stress them: what if two units sit empty, what if insurance goes up, what does the roof have left. If it still works, we make an offer.

Property types I work

Apartments, mixed use, retail, strip centers, warehouses, churches, dealerships, and special-use buildings. The process is the same. The buyer pool is what changes.
